About Frostail

Frostail is a plump caterpillar-like larva whose body is made up of eight shining blue ice-segments, each one slightly translucent with frozen water visible inside. Two small antennae curl forward over wide blue eyes, and its many tiny legs grip the frost-slick rocks with surprising security even when spray washes over it. It moves with a slow, measured rocking gait and spends much of its time sitting still, letting the spray freeze on its outer shell to build its next layer. Each segment is slightly colder than the one before it, measured from head to tail.

Field note: Frostail's ice-segments crack and re-seal each night with a sound like distant bells - a sound the peaks-folk recognise as the sign of a hard-frost morning.

About Glacail

Glacail has sealed itself inside a magnificent ice-crystal cocoon studded with a snowflake pattern and ringed with spines of aquamarine crystal. Inside, the slow work of transformation goes on - the cocoon holds its interior three degrees below the air around it, by a process no researcher has yet explained. Snow-hares and small birds shelter in the cocoon's lee during a thaw, using the pocket of hard cold it keeps as a refuge. The snowflake imprint on its shell is unique to each individual, like a fingerprint.

Field note: A Glacail cocoon found on the beach marks the precise point where the year's coldest bay-current will arrive, always within two days.

About Crystalyx

Crystalyx emerges from its cocoon as a stunning ice-wing butterfly whose four wings are patterned with perfect crystalline veining and shimmer with blue-white light. It flies low over the snowfields of Frozen Peaks, the chill of its wing-beats leaving brief frost-trails that catch the morning light. Small ice-crystals trail from its wingtips and settle on the rock, where they melt into mineral-rich water that feeds the cold-moss. Frozen Peaks children traditionally say winter has arrived the moment they spot the first Crystalyx of the season.

Field note: Crystalyx's wingbeat produces a frequency that keeps cold-moss from dying back within a five-meter radius - field researchers actively chart its flight corridors.
