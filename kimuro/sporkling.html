About Sporkling

Sporkling is a small round tortoise-like creature with a smooth spotted mushroom cap growing from its shell, its underside a warm earthy brown. It moves slowly along the sandy amber flats, leaving faint spore trails that glow faint yellow in sunlight. Though it looks gentle, the spores it releases are mildly toxic to plants - any flower that absorbs them wilts within the hour and regrows as something tougher. The guardians of Amber Desert call Sporkling a hidden test: the plants it visits either die or become stronger.

Field note: Every plant Sporkling visits either withers completely or grows back twice as hardy within a week - there is no middle result.

About Mycodon

Mycodon has grown warty and wide, its mushroom-cap shell now bristling with raised nodules that secrete a sticky amber resin. A broad squat face peers out from under the cap with hooded eyes that rarely fully open. When threatened, Mycodon presses its belly flat to the desert floor and puffs its cap, releasing a choking spore cloud that makes intruders stagger and then run. Beneath the amber resin on its cap, amber beetles make their nests - a mutual relationship where the beetles clean the cap and Mycodon protects the beetles from birds.

Field note: Mycodon can hold its breath for twenty-three minutes, which it uses to outlast the spore clouds it releases when it doesn't want to move.

About Sporgathor

Sporgathor is a formidable presence in the Amber Desert - a vast flat-capped tortoise whose shell has crusted over with thick yellow-green spore patches that pulse visibly every few minutes. Its four legs are dense as tree stumps, and deep furrows in the amber sand mark its ancient routes between the desert's hidden water seeps. The spore patches on its shell can be seen from a kilometer away, warning other creatures to give it a wide berth. Birds that dare to land on its shell and peck at its spores risk a direct spore-blast and are rarely seen twice.

Field note: Sporgathor's spore blooms on a 28-day cycle that perfectly matches the lunar calendar - desert communities use its blooms to track the months.
