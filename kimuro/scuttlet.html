About Scuttlet

Scuttlet is a small brown armadillo with a neatly banded shell and a soft pointed snout it keeps pressed to the sand at all times, reading the ground for vibrations. It lives in the upper dune layer where loose sand shifts constantly, and its banded shell acts as a natural sand-sieve - grains fill the gaps and make it effectively invisible when still. It rolls into a perfect ball at any perceived threat and bounces gently down dune slopes, which appears to be accidental but happens so consistently that researchers now believe it is deliberate.

Field note: Scuttlet rolled down the same dune 21 times in a single afternoon before researchers realized it was doing it on purpose, not out of fear.

About Armadex

Armadex has compacted itself into a near-perfect sphere of overlapping grey-stone plates - when curled, it is almost perfectly round, and on the steeper dunes it gains real speed in this form. Uncurled, it stands on four solid legs with a wide rounded body and moves with careful, deliberate steps. Its shell plates have taken on a stone-grey color now that it has grown past the lighter brown of youth, and each band is separated by a visible dark groove. Dune merchants mark territorial boundaries using Armadex's roll-tracks - the lines are always perfectly straight.

Field note: Armadex's rolled form achieves a near-perfect sphere within 0.4 seconds of curling - faster than the eye can track.

About Thanatusk

Thanatusk stands wide and solid, its grey stone-plate body bearing a thick spiked tail it drags behind it in the sand - the trail it leaves looks like a plow has gone through. Its large claws press deep footprints into packed dune rock, and when it shakes its body, the plates clatter against each other like a rockslide in miniature. The Sand Dunes' oldest trading routes follow paths Thanatusk has walked for generations - its weight compacts the sand into something firm enough to drive a cart over. The guardians called it the Road-Maker.

Field note: Thanatusk's tail-spike drag creates furrows up to 30 centimetres deep in packed dune sand - some are used as irrigation channels by dune farmers.
