About Burmite

Burmite is a small, perfectly round green toad with a single leaf-bud antenna bouncing from its head. It has wide shiny eyes and a calm, settled posture - it sits on the warm sand of the dunes like a little green stone with opinions. The antenna flickers up in dry heat, drawing a tiny drop of moisture from the air. Dune travellers say stumbling on a Burmite means there is groundwater close beneath your feet.

Field note: Burmite's antenna can sense underground moisture up to two metres down - desert guides treat it like a living well-finder.

About Burmoth

Burmoth has grown wide and solid, its smooth green hide now speckled with sandy earth tones. Three white-tipped antennae crown its head, bobbing in the desert breeze, and its sturdy legs end in broad flat pads that let it walk on loose sand without sinking. It digs shallow burrows each night and fills them with dew collected on its antennae. The blooms it leaves behind mark safe overnight stops for those who travel the Sand Dunes.

Field note: Each Burmoth fills three small burrows with dew-water overnight - small desert creatures follow it like a moving oasis.

About Burmatog

Burmatog is a wide, heavy toad whose entire back has become a living garden of green spines and pale bloom-pods that release a faint sweet pollen on the desert wind. Its broad flat body presses close to the earth, and each slow step sends a vibration through the sand that is felt twenty paces away. When it calls at dusk, every plant within earshot unfurls a bloom in answer - as though the desert is responding to a word it has been waiting a very long time to hear.

Field note: Burmatog calls at dusk every day without fail; botanists have confirmed the sand-bloom cycle follows its cry to the minute.
