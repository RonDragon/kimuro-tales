About Pebble

Pebble is a compact little tortoise whose shell is made of rough grey stone segments rather than the smooth keratin plates most turtles wear. Small irregular chunks of rock jut from the shell's surface like a geological sample come to life. It is extraordinarily heavy for its size - two strong children cannot lift it when it plants itself. It roams the Amber Desert slowly, feeding on mineral-rich soil it digs with wide flat forelimbs, and during the hottest part of the day it simply stops moving and becomes effectively invisible among the amber rocks.

Field note: Pebble has never once been successfully moved against its will - even the strongest desert winds cannot budge it when it presses down flat.

About Stonite

Stonite has grown considerably, and its shell has erupted into a field of upright stone spikes that range from stubby to alarmingly sharp. Its thick neck can swing its head in a full arc, and its broad feet are now equipped with flattened granite-hard claws for traction on steep rock faces. Stonite nests in the ancient petrified wood formations at the desert's edge, where the hard wood and its own spikes create a nearly impenetrable defensive ring. Sandstorm researchers have noted that dunes reform around Stonite resting sites rather than burying them - somehow it always ends up on top.

Field note: Stonite shells are collected by desert communities after they are shed - each shed shell is hard enough to use as a cutting board indefinitely.

About Crystallith

Crystallith in the Amber Desert is a different legend than the one of the Crystal Mines - this one is wider, older, and slower. Its shell has become a landscape feature: a vast armored plateau covered in meter-tall stone spikes, with desert lichen colonizing the spaces between. Four legs like stone pillars move it with geological patience across the amber sands. When it finally settles in a spot, it may remain for decades, and the dunes build up around it into new formations. The word for "patience" in the desert tongue is borrowed from the name for Crystallith's walking pace.

Field note: The largest known Crystallith has been tracked crossing the same desert since before living memory - locals say their great-grandparents mapped its route, and it has not changed.
